
They never quoted you the same thing.

Three people, three readings of your idea, three prices. They differ because of what each one knows, what each one assumes, and what each one is protecting against.

The gap between them isn't dishonesty. It's ambiguity.

### What a Quote Actually is

A quote isn't the price of the work. It's the price of the work _as that developer understood it_, plus a premium for everything they couldn't see that might come up later on.

Nobody ever understands your idea the same way, even if you present it with the exact same slides, words, and scenarios. 

Each person tends to fill in gaps with their own assumptions:
- What "simple" means 
- Whether your idea is one system or actually two
- Whether part 1 can be done now, while part 2 needs to be scoped separately
- Whether the data you need for it is clean or needs to be filtered.

Then they priced based on their assumptions.

Most of these never get said out loud in a meeting. They come out later, as a number.

### Lessons
Those are harsh realities. Two things follow.
#### **Uncertainty is expensive**
A developer who isn't sure what they're walking into either adds money or adds time. Both are the same thing: a hedge. 

The $235 quote is often "I'll figure it out as I go," which works until it doesn't. 

The $1,567 quote is "I'll assume the worst and protect myself." 

Neither is a villain. Both quotes are rational answers to a vague brief.
#### **The cheapest quote is frequently the most expensive** 
Not because the developer is bad — because the work they didn't price comes back later as a change request, at the moment you have the least leverage to argue.

### Personal Experience
I've been the developer in that gap and it cost myself money doing it. 

A hands-on, semi-technical founder on a five-month platform build. He didn't hand me a vague brief — he wrote the specifics himself. Screens, fields, flows, rules.

It was careful work and it still translated badly. His specs were written in his vocabulary. I built what the words said; he meant something adjacent. Every one of those rounds cost days, and the days came out of the five-month clock.

I should have pushed back the first time I guessed at what he meant. That was the honest move, and it would have cost me the engagement. I built it anyway.

The engagement ended later over rate, not over the build. The rate bought one engineer's time and nothing else — no scoping, no change process, no handover. I took it for the experience. That's the same mistake as the cheapest quote, seen from the other side of the table.

That's the trap. **Detail is not definition.**

### The five things a quote is priced against
1. **How defined the scope is.** A vague idea always carries a risk premium.
2. **Unknowns.** Existing code, third-party APIs, the state of your data.
3. **Who absorbs change.** Fixed price means they price in the changes they can't see yet.
4. **Their calendar.** A developer with three months booked quotes high to decline politely.
5. **Risk appetite.** Some bake in breakage. Some gamble. The gamble is not a discount.

A solo developer and a five-person agency can quote differently due to scale. This is a different story. Ask what you're paying for and decide if you want it.

### Where I Stand
I'm a developer. I've never been the one buying — never held three quotes and one paragraph of an idea, with no way to tell which number is real.

What I know is the other end of the table. I've filled the gaps with my own assumptions, sometimes because they were reasonable and sometimes because I needed the work. Some of those guesses turned out fine. Some cost me weeks, and cost the client more. Either way it put food on the table, whether or not I regret the rate now.

I've changed how I work since. If it can't be done, I say it can't. If the brief isn't clear enough to price, I say that instead of inventing a number. That loses me clients. It'll cost me again the next time work dries up and I'm hungry enough to guess. When I'm hungry, I'm the $235 quote. Now you know what that looks like from the inside.

So take this as a description of how the number gets built on my end. It isn't a buyer's playbook. The ambiguity is yours to close — but it isn't only yours to name. A developer who doesn't ask you hard questions is choosing an expensive silence. Asking costs them the engagement. Guessing costs you the calendar.
