title: "Don't let your vendor be your landlord"
slug: /dont-let-your-vendor-be-your-landlord
---

A landlord holds the keys. The lease is worthless if you can't get in the door.

Four things should be in your company's name _before_ anyone writes code — not after.

#### The repository
The git history, in your account or your org's. If it lives in a personal GitHub, you have every commit and none of the machinery — the issues, the CI, the deploy keys, the pull requests that explain why. And the next commit isn't yours.

#### The domain
Whoever holds the registrar account holds your company's name. One expired card, one falling-out, and your email, your site and your invoices go with it. DNS doesn't care about your relationship.

#### The cloud account
Their card, their account, your data. When the relationship ends, so does your access — usually suddenly, usually mid-quarter.

#### The credentials
Every password, key and token in a shared vault you control. Not a spreadsheet emailed at the end. The end of a project is exactly when nobody feels like tidying up.

### The ask
You don't have to hold everything — hold recovery, billing and the override; give them a scoped key you can revoke in a click.

Nobody thinks about this until their developer gets run over by a bus. Least of all the bus. So the ask is: _"If you got hit by a bus, I need to keep running."_ Nobody argues with the bus.

Week one, this is diligence. Month nine, it's the insult — which is why the people who ask late are usually the ones who were worried all along.

Anyone who pushes back is telling you something. Believe them — or get a date and a signed handover in writing. **If I ever push back, do the same.**

Four things, all in your company's name. Not your vendor's. Not yours either.

Then you can be as trusting as you like.

Already wrong? Start with the domain. Cheapest to move, most expensive to lose.
